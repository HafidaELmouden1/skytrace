02 · The scientific challenge

SPHEREx maps the entire sky every six months, in 102 near-infrared bands.

  • Since 2025, NASA's SPHEREx telescope sweeps the whole sky in 102 narrow bands between 0.75 and 5 µm — more than a billion objects per survey.
  • Each band isolates different molecules — water, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, hydrogen-rich dust — the chemistry of clouds, disks and early galaxies.
  • Because the survey repeats every six months, two images of the same sky form a time series: anything that moved, brightened or faded becomes measurable.

The challenge, in NASA's words

“Create a public-facing web tool to display images of the sky from the SPHEREx mission, making it easy for anyone to quickly see how they change over time.”

03 · The problem

The images are public. Comparing them is not.

A SPHEREx exposure is a scientific instrument readout, not a photograph. Looking at one tells you nothing about change.

  • Volume. 1.63 million calibrated exposures in IRSA across DR1 and QR3. Nobody browses that by hand.
  • Format. A multi-extension FITS: 2040 × 2040 float32 pixels, a WCS solution and a flags bitmap — 16.6 MB of raw array before any display.
  • Two releases, one sky. DR1 covers 2025, QR3 covers 2026. Use one alone and most of the sky shows a single epoch.
  • No comparison layer. Archive tools answer “show me this image”. Almost none answer “what changed between these two dates?” — the exact question the challenge asks.

04 · Our solution

ASTRAVIEW — three modules, all running on real SPHEREx files.

astraview.cloud.run / explorer

1 · Explorer live

Search 1 513 indexed Level-2 observations by sky position or object name, band (D1–D6), release (DR1 / QR3), field size and date window. Every card carries ObsID, sky centre, UTC time and the 2040 × 2040 exposure grid.

2 · Time Machine live

36 verified multi-epoch pairs, up to 381 days apart. Smart slider, side by side or blink — aligned on sky coordinates in the same band, so the stars sit on top of each other.

3 · Cosmic Signals live

Pixel-by-pixel After − Before, noise measured from the difference itself (σ = 0.0156 MJy/sr). Each candidate is labelled “Unverified” — never sold as a discovery.

A Nothing to install, nothing to request

IRSA SIA2, IBE and the NASA S3 mirror are open endpoints — no API key, no token, no account. The whole pipeline runs from a browser tab.

B FITS is parsed server-side, not in a desktop app

A TypeScript multi-extension parser reads headers and the image HDU straight from the archive — including S3 byte ranges — encodes a display PNG and ships pixels, so a phone can view a 16 MB array.

C Deployed, not demonstrated on a laptop

The same build runs in a container on Google Cloud Run at astraview.cloud.run, with a /api/health probe reporting which archive path is answering.

06 · Innovation, impact and team

Built by three developers, for anyone curious about the sky.

Accessible by design

A real 2040 × 2040 NASA array, stretched and colour-mapped for the eye, with the sky coordinate under the cursor. No download, no astronomy degree, no install.

Honest by design

Every difference is measured against its own noise, labelled “Potential Candidate — Unverified”, and paired with the list of artifacts that could fake it.

Reusable by design

The indexer, TAN-SIP alignment and difference detector work on any survey that images the same sky more than once — SPHEREx is the first, not the last.

Shipped this weekend

  • ✓Explorer over 1 513 indexed observations — 6 bands, 2 releases, date windows.
  • ✓Time Machine with 36 verified pairs and three comparison modes.
  • ✓Cosmic Signals with noise-based candidate ranking and a disclaimer.
  • ✓Public deployment on Cloud Run — no keys, no account.
